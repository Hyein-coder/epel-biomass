# 3.1. Scale-up and feedstock price

The cheapest plant size follows from two opposing forces: scale lowers conversion cost with sharply diminishing returns, while a realistic feedstock supply curve raises biomass cost as the plant grows. Their crossover fixes the minimum-MJSP scale at x10 (0.457 MMt/yr).

![Base-case MJSP breakdown vs SAF production scale](../Figure/scale_up_wo_price_model_base_summary.png)

**Step 1 — Scale lowers MJSP, but the return fades by ~x10.** Scaling the base process from 0.04 to 1.25 MMt/yr SAF cuts MJSP to a third, \$1.46 → \$0.54/L SAF.

- Co-product revenue exceeds raw-material cost at every scale, so MJSP recovers only conversion cost — capital, utilities, fixed operations — which are exactly the scale-sensitive components. Capital investment supplies about half the reduction up to 0.42 MMt/yr, utilities about a quarter, and fixed operating costs (labor, maintenance, overhead, G&amp;A) most of the rest.
- The return is front-loaded: each capacity doubling cuts MJSP ~11% below 0.42 MMt/yr but only ~3% above it, so 0.42 MMt/yr already captures ~90% of the total reduction available up to 1.25 MMt/yr. The capital scaling exponent rises from ~0.8 to ~1.0 across that point as the pyrolyzer, gas turbines, compressors, and expanders hit single-train limits and multiply into parallel trains (Table 5).
- This defines the minimum efficient size (MES): ~0.42 MMt/yr, about 10% of South Korea's domestic aviation-fuel demand. (The MES hinges on modeling those single-train limits — a uniform 0.6 exponent would instead project ~\$0.25/L and favor ever-larger plants.)

![MJSP vs SAF production under three biomass price models (bars = biomass price; lines = MJSP for the grey-H2 base, full green-ammonia, and full CCS)](../Figure/scale_up_price_model_mjsp.png)

**Step 2 — Feedstock price, flat above, turns against larger scales.** Step 1 held biomass at the forest-residue floor (\$0.0486/lb); real supply curves raise it once demand outgrows the local low-cost resource. Three models bracket the effect: **FR Price** (constant floor), **FR–Log Linear**, and **Fixed Elasticity** (the latter two rising curves fitted to Galik forest-residue and log resources).

- Below ~0.4 MMt/yr all three coincide at the floor: demand stays inside the forest-residue supply, feedstock price is scale-invariant, and the Step 1 curves are unchanged.
- At x10 (0.457 MMt/yr) the penalty is still small — biomass rises \$0.0486 → \$0.0527/lb (+8.3%), adding only ~\$0.06/L (base 0.78 → 0.84 \$/L).
- Beyond x10 the penalty reverses the scale benefit: at 0.913 MMt/yr base MJSP climbs 0.70 → 1.28 (log-linear) / 1.50 (fixed elasticity), and at 1.37 MMt/yr 0.66 → 1.76 / 3.26, versus the flat-price curve still falling to 0.66. Biomass is shared, so CCS and ammonia shift by the same delta.

**Step 3 — The two forces cross at x10, making it the cheapest scale.** Below x10 scale cuts cost faster than feedstock rises; above x10 feedstock rises faster than scale saves. Under any rising supply curve the net MJSP minimum therefore sits at x10 (0.457 MMt/yr) — only the flat-price model, which ignores supply-curve escalation, keeps favoring bigger plants.

- This reinforces numbering-up over a single mega-plant: several MES-sized plants, each on its own local feedstock base, cost only ~6% more than one 1.25 MMt/yr plant while avoiding both the supply-curve penalty and the haulage cost (excluded here) that grow with size, so the practical optimum lies at or below 0.42 MMt/yr.
- At this scale operating expenditure is over 80% of gross production cost, so the remaining cost and emission levers lie in hydrogen supply and process configuration, not plant size. The base case runs on grey hydrogen and exceeds the SAF emission threshold, making ~\$1.00/L a lower bound for compliant fuel; §3.2 examines how much of the scale advantage survives green-ammonia and CCS integration, and whether the MES shifts once they are added.



# 3.2. Green ammonia and CCS integration

![MJSP by production scale for the grey-H2 base, full green-ammonia, and 60% CCS configurations](../Figure/scale_up_mjsp_bar.png)

- Replacing grey hydrogen with green ammonia raises MJSP by about $1.7–1.8/L SAF at every scale, and this premium barely narrows with size (3.43 → 2.40 $/L SAF over 0.046 → 1.37 MMt/yr, against 1.58 → 0.68 for the base).
  - The ammonia-cracked hydrogen cost dominates the case, so scaling the plant does not close the gap. Green ammonia never reaches cost parity with grey hydrogen; its value is emissions depth, not cheaper fuel.
- Adding 60% CCS to the grey-H2 base carries a nearly flat +$0.16/L SAF premium that is independent of scale (1.74 → 0.84 $/L SAF, tracking the base curve down).
  - Capture cost is proportional to the CO2 captured, which itself scales with plant size, so — unlike whole-plant capital — the capture unit earns no economy of scale. CCS lowers absolute MJSP only by riding the base scale-up curve, not by becoming cheaper per tonne captured.
- Because both add-ons are scale-invariant relative to the base (grey-H2 variable cost sets a hard floor, and the CCS premium is constant), neither shifts the MES: the ~0.42 MMt/yr knee of §3.1 still governs. Integrating green ammonia or CCS changes the level of MJSP, not the scale at which further scale-up stops paying.

- WTWa GHG-100 intensity is fixed by the life-cycle inventory per MJ of SAF and is therefore scale-independent; total annual emissions scale linearly with production. The per-subunit intensities (allocation method) are:
  - Grey-H2 base: 87.6 g CO2e/MJ — above the CORSIA threshold (75.9 g CO2e/MJ, 0.9× conventional jet), so the base fuel is non-compliant at any scale.
  - Full green ammonia: 49.5 g CO2e/MJ, a 44% cut versus base — the deeper of the two levers.
  - 60% CCS: 60.3 g CO2e/MJ, a 31% cut. Both integrated configurations clear the CORSIA threshold at every scale, whereas the base does not.

- At the MES (~0.42 MMt/yr) the plant emits ≈1.8 Mt CO2e/yr on grey hydrogen; full green ammonia brings this to ≈1.0 Mt/yr and 60% CCS to ≈1.2 Mt/yr. Scaling on to 1.37 MMt/yr triples the absolute burden (5.4 / 3.0 / 3.7 Mt/yr). The emissions case for the hydrogen-supply and CCS levers therefore strengthens in absolute terms exactly where the scale-up economics flatten, reinforcing that the remaining decarbonization levers lie in configuration rather than plant size.
