# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Process design

### 2.1.1 Main process unit

The process is a biomass fast-pyrolysis-to-fuels flowsheet adapted from the NREL/PNNL
design of Jones et al. (2013). The Aspen Plus flowsheet is costed in Aspen Process
Economic Analyzer (APEA); the resulting *Main* block (whole-flowsheet CAPEX/OPEX read
from the APEA Project Summary sheet) is combined with five literature-interpolated
sub-units (CCS, PSA hydrogen recovery, off-gas reformer, hydrotreating catalysts, and
the R-510 hydrocracker; cost interpolation detailed in SI §S2). The flowsheet is organized into eight areas: feed handling,
pyrolysis (reactor R-110, area 100), hydrotreating (area 200), product finishing,
hydrogen recovery, hydrocracking (R-510, area 500), off-gas combustion, and the
steam/balance-of-plant island.

Four changes distinguish this design from the Jones et al. (2013) base case:

- **Product slate.** The plant is retuned to maximize sustainable aviation fuel (SAF,
  stream 326SAF) for the South Korean market rather than the gasoline/diesel blendstock
  of the original design. Naphtha-range (316GAS) and diesel-range (336DIES) streams are
  retained as priced co-products.
- **Power co-product.** A gas-turbine power island (ELECGEN) is added so that surplus
  electricity is exported as a co-product, absent from the Jones base case.
- **Hydrogen-supply flexibility.** The grey hydrogen of the original design can be
  replaced, in whole or in part, by green-ammonia-cracked hydrogen or SMR-with-CCS
  hydrogen (§2.1.2, §2.2.2).
- **Carbon capture.** An amine CCS retrofit is added on the recoverable CO₂ streams
  (§2.1.3).

Base-case feed is 0.343 MMt/yr of woody biomass, producing 0.041 MMt/yr of SAF
(stream 326SAF); the bio-oil sent to hydrotreating is 0.244 MMt/yr (≈71 wt% of the biomass). Fresh make-up hydrogen totals 0.0238 MMt/yr
(0.0212 MMt/yr to hydrotreating stream 250H2, 0.00268 MMt/yr to hydrocracking stream 532H2).
Hydrotreating uses ruthenium catalysts in reactors R-202 and R-220 (LHSV 0.5) and a
molybdenum catalyst in R-225 (LHSV 0.22); catalyst charging is costed separately from
the vessels.

**Scale-up and single-train limits.** Capacity is scaled from the base case (0.041 MMt/yr SAF) up to 1.23 MMt/yr SAF. Rather than applying a single blanket cost exponent, each major unit carries an explicit single-train ceiling and is numbered up into parallel trains once that ceiling is reached. The per-unit single-train caps  
and the resulting replica counts are tabulated in SI Table S3-1; because each unit is  
replicated into identical parallel trains rather than enlarged, the added trains capture  
none of the single-unit economies of scale, which is the mechanism driving the exponent  
toward 1.0 above the minimum efficient scale. This train-level detail is realized inside the  
per-scale assessments, not by a closed-form correlation. 

All costs are reported in first-quarter 2022 USD; Jones-era (2011) source costs are
escalated by the cost-index ratio 3230/2250. Financial basis: 8000 operating hours per
year, a 30-year economic life, a 10% internal rate of return, and a 35% tax rate. The
full cost basis and financial parameters are tabulated in SI §S1.

### 2.1.2 Ammonia cracker

Green ammonia is used as a hydrogen carrier: imported NH₃ is catalytically cracked to  
supply make-up hydrogen.  The cracker is sized to the plant's make-up hydrogen demand. Cracker reactors carry a single-train ceiling of 403 GWh/yr. The cracking reaction network (GHSV, kinetics) follows the source used for the Aspen model (Int. J. Hydrogen Energy, 2024); ammonia conversion and  
hydrogen recovery are embedded in that simulation rather than imposed as fixed constants. A nitrogen-fertilizer stream is credited as an ammonia-side co-product. The cracker process is described in detail in SI §S4. 

### 2.1.3 Carbon capture and storage (CCS)

CO₂ capture is a post-combustion amine (MEA) retrofit, cost-interpolated from the
Rao & Rubin (2002) power-plant amine-capture base case (base stream 3.13 MMt/yr CO₂;
total CAPEX $297.9 M; regeneration heat 7772 GWh/yr; auxiliary electricity at 14% of
the heat duty, all rebased to 1Q-2022 USD). *(The engine comment labels this "Rao,
2022"; the intended and verified reference is Rao, A. B.; Rubin, E. S. Environ. Sci.
Technol. 2002, 36 (20), 4467–4475.)*

The captured mass is `co2_seq = ccs_portion × (623CO2) + (622X)`: the fraction
`ccs_portion` of the recoverable process CO₂ stream (623CO2) plus the EGR CO₂ stream
(622X), which is always fully captured. Capture-unit CAPEX scales with the captured mass
by the six-tenths rule (exponent 0.6), OPEX by exponent 0.7, and utility draws linearly.
Capture OPEX comprises make-up fuel for any regeneration heat not met by on-site heat,
auxiliary electricity, and optional CO₂ transport-and-storage (T&S; §2.2.3). The capture
portion is swept from 0 to 1.0. The full CCS cost derivation is given in SI §S5.

## 2.2 Techno-economic analysis

The economic metric is the minimum jet-fuel selling price (MJSP, $/L SAF), obtained from
a discounted-cash-flow calculation of the SAF price that zeroes net present value over
the 30-year life at a 10% IRR and 35% tax, with straight-line depreciation and zero
salvage. Equivalently, MJSP = (minimum required revenue − co-product sales) / annual SAF
volume. Co-product sales (naphtha, diesel, exported electricity, ammonia-side fertilizer)
are credited on the cost side. Utility prices: electricity 65.0 $/MWh (≈0.065 $/kWh),
natural gas 49.1 $/MWh.

### 2.2.1 Feedstock price model

Because domestic forest-residue supply is finite, the delivered biomass price is treated
as a function of plant scale rather than a fixed input — a larger plant must draw on
progressively costlier feedstock (the regional supply-availability context is in SI §S8).
Biomass demand is mapped to scale as
demand = scale × 0.330 MMt/yr, and three supply-curve models are compared:

- **FR Price (reference).** A constant forest-residue price of 107.3 $/t, independent
  of scale — the optimistic floor.
- **FR–Log Linear** (naive Galik supply curve). The forest-residue price holds until
  regional forest-residue supply (2.84 MMt/yr) is exhausted, beyond which the delivered
  price rises linearly with demand, price = supply × 25.08 + 33.30 $/t (supply in MMt/yr), as
  logging residue is drawn in.
- **Fixed Elasticity** (Galik supply curve, supply elasticity 0.3). Follows the
  FR–Log Linear curve until the combined forest-residue-plus-log supply (6.12 MMt/yr)
  is exhausted, then extrapolates linearly under a fixed supply elasticity of 0.3 (log
  reference price 192.4 $/t), giving the steepest scale penalty.

**Scale-up evaluation.** MJSP is evaluated at seven plant scales, 0.041, 0.104, 0.207,
0.311, 0.414, 0.828 and 1.243 MMt/yr SAF. At the forest-residue floor price, the
base-case (grey-H₂) MJSP at each scale is broken down into capital investment, raw
materials, utilities, fixed operating costs (operating labor, maintenance, operating
charges, plant overhead, and general and administrative costs) and co-product sales,
which enter as a negative term (SI Table S9-1). Each of the three supply-curve models
then sets the delivered biomass price at each scale, and MJSP is recomputed for three
configurations: the grey-H₂ base, full green ammonia, and full CCS (SI Tables S9-2 and
S9-3). Biomass is costed identically in all three configurations, so a given price model
shifts each of them by the same biomass-cost increment. The minimum-MJSP scale is read
where the falling conversion cost and the rising feedstock cost cross.

### 2.2.2 Green ammonia and hydrogen supply

Green ammonia is priced at 635 $/t NH₃ (base), swept over 270 $/t (projected,
future) to 1000 $/t (current). Ammonia-cracked hydrogen substitutes grey hydrogen at
the `ammonia_portion` fraction; the cost-and-emissions optimum used in the analysis is
≈0.655. The grey-hydrogen reference is SMR-with-CCS costed from Katebah (2022)
(1499 $/t H₂).

### 2.2.3 CCS cost

Capture cost follows §2.1.3. CO₂ transport and storage is applied to the sequestered
mass at a base rate of 10 $/t CO₂ (sensitivity range 4–45 $/t CO₂); the T&S cost adder
is linear in the T&S price.

### 2.2.4 Sensitivity analysis

Five complementary sensitivity treatments are run:

- **Tornado.** Eight parameters are perturbed one at a time for three configurations
  (grey-H₂ base, full green ammonia, full CCS) at both the smallest and largest scale:
  biomass price ±50%, grey-H₂ price 1000–4000 $/t, ammonia price 270–1000 $/t,
  CAPEX ±30%, CO₂ T&S 4–45 $/t (CCS only), electricity price ±30%, co-product price
  ±20%, and capacity factor 85%/91%. Price-like parameters are scaled analytically about
  the base run; the rest are re-run with the corresponding knob reset.
- **Pareto front.** With the main plant fixed at the knee-point scale (0.41 MMt/yr SAF), the
  ammonia portion (0–1) and CCS portion (0–1.0) are gridded into 30 configurations, and
  the non-dominated set is found minimizing both MJSP and WTWa GHG-100 intensity. Each
  point carries an MJSP uncertainty band drawn from the tornado drivers.
  The 6 × 5 grid crosses green-NH₃ hydrogen shares of 0, 10, 25, 50, 75 and 100% with CCS
  portions of 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100% (SI Table S9-4). MJSP is computed by the full TEA at
  every grid point. GHG is reported on the allocation basis with the Korea grid mix
  (§2.3.2). It is built from the simulated CCS cases and a constant ammonia increment,
  GHG(a, c) = GHG_CCS(c) − a × (GHG_grey − GHG_NH₃), where a is the ammonia portion and c
  the CCS portion. This assumes that the upstream emission avoided by replacing grey H₂ is
  independent of, and additive to, the process CO₂ captured by CCS. In the band, biomass
  price is swept over the supply-curve span at this scale (107.3–116.1 \$/t, the
  forest-residue floor to the supply-curve price; §2.2.1) instead of the ±50% tornado
  range. CCS 60%, the cheapest CORSIA-compliant point, lies between grid levels. Its MJSP
  comes from a TEA run at a CCS portion of 0.60, and its GHG is interpolated linearly
  between the simulated CCS 50% and 75% cases (SI §S7).
- **CO₂ abatement cost.** Computed as ΔMJSP × 10⁶ / (ΔGHG × E), where E = 31.66 MJ/L is
  the volumetric energy of SAF, giving $/t CO₂ avoided relative to the grey-H₂ base.
  In the Pareto analysis the reference point is CCS 60% rather than the grey-H₂ base. A
  producer must reach compliance at least, so the reported value is the extra cost of
  abatement below that compliance point. The cost is calculated only for configurations
  with lower GHG intensity than CCS 60%; configurations at or above it are left undefined.
  The values are plotted as a contour over green-NH₃ share and CCS portion (SI Table S9-4).
- **SHAP attribution.** The two cheapest compliant routes, CCS 60% and Green NH₃ 10% +
  CCS 50%, are examined with all drivers varied jointly. The tornado drivers and ranges
  are reused, except that biomass takes the supply-curve band above. CCS 60% has seven
  drivers, because green-NH₃ price is inactive at a zero ammonia share. Green NH₃ 10% +
  CCS 50% has eight. For each route, 128 Latin-hypercube samples over the driver ranges are
  evaluated with the TEA. A gradient-boosted regression surrogate is fitted to them
  (in-sample R² > 0.99 is required), and TreeSHAP on the surrogate attributes each sample's
  MJSP to its drivers. MJSP is close to additive in these drivers, so the surrogate tracks
  the TEA almost exactly, and its fit is the only approximation in the attribution. Drivers
  are ranked by mean |SHAP| (SI Table S9-6). One-at-a-time MJSP spans over the same ranges
  are computed for comparison (SI Table S9-7).
- **NH₃-vs-CCS crossover.** The MJSP of the two compliant routes is expressed as a function
  of grey-H₂ price (1–4 \$/kg). Each MJSP term is linear in its own price driver, so each
  route is a straight line. The line passes through the route's base MJSP at the base
  grey-H₂ price (1.50 \$/kg), and its slope is the one-at-a-time grey-H₂ span divided by
  the 3 \$/kg width of the range. CCS 60% uses only grey H₂, while the NH₃ route still
  takes 90% of its hydrogen from grey H₂, so the CCS-only line is steeper. The NH₃ route
  is drawn for several green-NH₃ prices (270, 441, 635 and 1000 \$/t). At each price its
  line is offset by 10% of the full-ammonia ammonia-cost term, scaled by the relative change
  in ammonia price. The crossover is the grey-H₂ price at which the two lines meet (SI Table
  S9-5). These lines are built analytically from the recorded base values and spans; no
  extra TEA runs are made.

## 2.3 Life-cycle assessment

### 2.3.1 System boundary and biogenic carbon

The functional unit is 1 MJ of SAF and the boundary is well-to-wake (WTWa), taken as the
sum of well-to-pump (WTP) and pump-to-wake (PTWa, i.e. combustion) stages; the combustion
stage is taken from the Fischer–Tropsch-jet reference. Impacts are reported as GHG-100
(100-year global warming potential) in g CO₂e per MJ SAF, using GREET-derived
inventories; the inventory tables, allocation factors, and SAF criterion are compiled in
SI §S7.

Biogenic carbon is treated under carbon neutrality: biogenic combustion CO₂ is carried as
a separate species (CO₂_Biogenic) distinct from fossil CO₂ so that the biomass uptake and
its combustion release net to zero, while any biogenic CO₂ diverted to CCS registers as a
negative emission. A time-resolved (dynamic) LCA variant is also available, propagating
each pulse through the Bern impulse-response carbon-climate model (Levasseur, 2010) with a
sequestration trajectory (Lan, 2021); it is used for the dynamic-GWP illustration only,
not for the headline intensities (model detail in SI §S6).

### 2.3.2 Allocation

Co-product emissions are handled by allocation, which is used for the central estimate.
Displacement (system-expansion credit) is computed alongside it, and the two bracket the
reported value: the central intensity is the allocation result and the reported spread is
the allocation-to-displacement range. This choice keeps the SAF intensity insensitive to
volatile displacement credits (grid electricity, fertilizer) whose market values swing the
displacement result far more than the allocation result — the allocation basis therefore
gives the more stable and conservative headline number, with displacement retained as the
uncertainty bound. Co-products carried in the allocation are exported electricity and, in
the ammonia configurations, nitrogen fertilizer. Both U.S.-grid and Korea-grid electricity
mixes are evaluated.

### 2.3.3 SAF compliance criterion

Compliance is judged against the CORSIA rule: an eligible SAF must emit at least 10% less
than the conventional-jet baseline on a WTWa GHG-100 basis, i.e. the threshold is
0.9 × (conventional-jet WTWa GHG-100). The conventional baseline is taken from the GREET
inventory. On this basis the grey-H₂ base case (87.6 g CO₂e/MJ) is non-compliant, while
full green ammonia (49.5 g CO₂e/MJ) and 60% CCS (60.3 g CO₂e/MJ) both clear the threshold.
These intensities use the U.S. grid mix. The cost–emission grid and the compliance
screening in the Results (§3.2) use the Korea grid mix instead. On that basis the
threshold is 75.93 g CO₂e/MJ and the grey-H₂ base emits 100.8 g CO₂e/MJ, while CCS 60%
emits 75.14 g CO₂e/MJ and is the cheapest configuration that complies (SI Table S9-4).
