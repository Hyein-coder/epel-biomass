% ¶1. Aviation needs a drop-in decarbonization route
Aviation decarbonization remains challenging, particularly for long-haul flights, which are difficult to electrify. Sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) offers a near-term pathway to reducing aviation emissions because it can be used in existing aircraft and fuelling infrastructure without requiring major modifications \citep{atsonios2015alternative, doliente2020bio}. This pathway is particularly relevant to the aviation industry's commitment to halve net CO$_2$ emissions by 2050 relative to 2005, following the adoption of this target by the International Air Transport Association (IATA). Under the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA), SAF must achieve life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions at least 10\% lower than those of conventional jet fuel to qualify for emissions reductions. 

% ¶2. Current SAF routes are limited; lignocellulosic pyrolysis is the alternative
However, existing SAF production pathways face constraints related to either feedstock availability or process requirements. Hydroprocessed esters and fatty acids (HEFA) relies on waste oils and fats, whose limited supply is projected to meet only about 70\% of SAF demand in 2030 \citep{doliente2020bio}; consequently, HEFA alone cannot keep pace with the growing demand for SAF. Gasification followed by Fischer--Tropsch synthesis, by contrast, can convert lignocellulosic feedstocks, but it requires high-temperature operation and extensive syngas cleanup to remove contaminants \citep{jones2013process}, which adds to both process complexity and cost \citep{swanson2010techno, anex2010techno}. These limitations motivate SAF pathways that can draw on more abundant feedstocks while operating under less demanding conditions.

Fast pyrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass offers such an alternative, because it operates at lower temperatures than gasification \citep{bridgwater2012review, swanson2010techno} and can utilize forest and agricultural residues, which account for more than 60\% of global biomass potential \citep{wang2019biomass}. In addition, lignin-derived compounds can contribute to the aromatic and cycloalkane fractions required in jet fuel \citep{son2022hybrid, escalante2022evaluation}.
Accordingly, earlier techno-economic and life-cycle studies have proposed a wide range of process designs for woody-biomass pyrolysis, differing in catalytic upgrading, hydrogen sourcing, and coproduct recovery, with minimum fuel selling prices of 34.8--91.0~\$/GJ (2022 USD, escalated using the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index) and life-cycle greenhouse gas emissions of 3.9--35.0~g~CO$_2$e/MJ \citep{dutta2023model, griffin2024opening}. Despite these advances, the resulting fuels remain considerably more expensive than petroleum-derived jet fuel, which limits their commercial deployment.

% ¶3. scale lowers conversion cost, but biomass supply pushes back
The economic viability of pyrolysis-based SAF production depends not only on conversion efficiency but also on the scale of the production facility and the cost of securing biomass feedstock. In particular, feedstock availability and cost remain key factors in the sustainable production of alternative jet fuels. Although increasing plant capacity can reduce unit capital costs, it also raises feedstock costs as sourcing expands beyond nearby low-cost resources \citep{chireshe2025cost}. Previous bioenergy studies have demonstrated that the interaction between conversion economies of scale and biomass supply costs can determine the cost-optimal facility size \citep{de2017cost}. Accordingly, assuming a constant feedstock price may overstate the economic benefits of plant expansion. This trade-off is particularly relevant to South Korea, where the economically usable supply of forest residues must be distinguished from the country's substantial forest area. Therefore, determining the cost-optimal scale of pyrolysis-based SAF production requires evaluating conversion costs against scale-dependent biomass supply costs under domestic resource constraints. 

% ¶4. Two levers with different cost structures: green ammonia and process CCS
Beyond plant scale, reducing the carbon intensity of pyrolysis-based SAF requires improvements in process efficiency and carbon management. Fast-pyrolysis bio-oil contains substantial oxygen and must undergo hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) and related upgrading steps to produce aviation-range hydrocarbons, adding hydrogen demand, energy consumption, and process emissions \citep{sorunmu2020review}. Therefore, process intensification through heat integration, improved conversion and product recovery, and post-combustion CO$_2$ capture and storage (CCS) can reduce the energy and emissions burdens of SAF production. However, the benefits of these measures depend on the process configuration and the extent of achievable emission reduction, motivating an integrated assessment of process design, energy use, and carbon capture rather than evaluating individual measures in isolation. 

The environmental performance of pyrolysis-based SAF also depends on the origin of the hydrogen required for bio-oil upgrading. Conventional steam methane reforming relies on natural gas and generates direct CO$_2$ emissions, while upstream methane emissions and incomplete capture can contribute to the life-cycle greenhouse gas footprint even when carbon capture is employed \citep{howarth2021green}. Green ammonia offers an alternative route for supplying renewable hydrogen, leveraging ammonia's potential for large-scale storage and transport while requiring additional energy for cracking and hydrogen recovery \citep{salmon2021green}. Comparing grey hydrogen with green-ammonia cracking under different post-combustion CO$_2$ capture configurations can thus establish how hydrogen sourcing and process-level carbon management jointly affect the economic and environmental performance of pyrolysis-based SAF. 

% ¶6. Objective and contributions
This study integrates techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessment (LCA) of a forest-residue fast-pyrolysis process for SAF to address two sequential questions: what plant capacity minimizes the minimum jet fuel selling price (MJSP) when biomass supply costs and equipment constraints are considered, and how should hydrogen supply and carbon capture be configured to meet the CORSIA emissions threshold at that capacity? The study makes three contributions. First, it identifies the cost-optimal plant capacity by accounting for single-train equipment limits and comparing three biomass pricing models: a constant price, a log-linear supply curve, and a fixed-elasticity supply curve. Second, at the selected capacity, it evaluates 30 configurations spanning the green-ammonia share of hydrogen supply and the post-combustion CO$_2$ capture rate, maps their costs and life-cycle emissions, identifies the lowest-cost configuration that meets the CORSIA threshold, and quantifies the cost of further emissions abatement. Third, it assesses the sensitivity of configuration rankings to price uncertainty using tornado and Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analyses, and identifies the grey-hydrogen price at which green-ammonia cracking becomes more cost-effective than CCS alone.
